# First Day Away — Research and Editorial Standard

Status: **authoritative for destination research and reader-facing country content.** The app
and production country compiler are not built. Iceland is the first strict-audited editorial
draft under this standard; its pending human and release gates are recorded in its review report.

First Day Away is an experience-led travel briefing. Its primary job is to help a traveler
understand what a destination will feel like and make consequential decisions before those
decisions become rushed, expensive or confusing. Language reference and optional practice
support that job.

## Product hierarchy

1. **Briefing:** a vivid, useful orientation to the destination.
2. **Playbooks:** decision support for the trip modes and recurring situations that matter
   there: for example self-driving, public transport, pools, clothing or payments.
3. **Experiences and practical routes:** ways to assemble places into a realistic trip.
4. **Language toolkit:** courtesy, signs, likely replies, emergencies and Show a local.
5. **Practice:** user-invoked recall and listening exercises. Practice never blocks the first
   four layers and does not interrupt them with compulsory questions.

The hierarchy is deliberate. Completion, quiz scores and streak-like mechanics are not the
primary measure of preparedness.

## The research question

Research starts with the target traveler's decisions, not a generic category inventory.
Ask:

- What surprised recent first-time visitors?
- What did they wish they had decided or prepared before arrival?
- What mistakes, near misses and avoidable costs recur?
- Which experiences were worth repeating, oversold or valuable only for a particular traveler?
- Where do travelers disagree, and which differences in season, route, budget, ability,
  party or risk tolerance explain it?
- Which local words, signs or replies became useful inside those experiences?

A useful pack is a function of destination, traveler origin, season and trip style. Keep the
destination evidence reusable, then apply an explicit audience lens such as US first-time
traveler, self-driver, no-car visitor, family, solo traveler or accessibility need.

## Travel-mode discovery

Discover how people actually travel in the destination before fixing the table of contents.
Search recent accounts broadly, then follow destination-specific modes such as compact
campervans, motorhomes, rail passes, ferries, cruises, domestic flights, bicycles, walking
routes or driver-guides. This is a discovery list, not a quota.

Separate a mode when it changes lodging, route flexibility, daily chores, reservations, cost,
accessibility, safety or legal constraints. “Self-drive” may therefore be too coarse: a compact
campervan can combine transport, bed and cooking while adding campsite dependence and confined
wet-weather living that a rental car plus lodging does not have.

For every candidate mode, record evidence of use, traveler fit, desired experience, advantages,
ordinary frictions, seasonality, official/provider checks and a decision to cover or exclude it.
Do not infer popularity from forum counts. The dossier may say that a mode recurs in the corpus;
a stronger market-share claim requires an appropriate source.

Research itinerary topology as a separate dimension. One base, two regional bases, frequent
lodging changes and a continuous circuit can use the same transport but create different
packing, recovery, backtracking and driver-fatigue decisions. Country content should surface
the destination-specific shapes that help, rather than equating self-drive with moving nightly.

Research season fit as a decision, not a weather table. “Worst month” is meaningful only for a
stated goal: broad road access, dark skies, wildlife, hiking, lower demand or winter atmosphere
may point to different answers. Explain the poor matches explicitly, retain counterexamples and
never present a monthly tendency as the trip's forecast. Clothing guidance must preserve an
all-season safety baseline and then say what insulation, footwear and small essentials change
for summer, shoulder season and winter.

When clothing or equipment is a material trip decision, cover `bring/buy before`, `rent there`
and `buy locally` where those options exist. Compare price, luggage, sizing, pickup and return,
expected reuse, quality and authenticity. A destination may produce excellent garments without
every souvenir-shop item being locally made; describe provenance labels readers can verify and
keep provider inventory and prices as live checks.

## Source roles

Do not flatten different sources into one undifferentiated citation list.

| Source role | What it can support | What it cannot establish alone |
|---|---|---|
| Firsthand traveler account | A real experience, concern, decision or outcome in its stated context | Population frequency, a current rule, or universal advice |
| Forum discussion | Recurrence, disagreement, vocabulary travelers use, questions needing answers | Incident rates or a representative sample |
| Travel program, guide or blog | Topic discovery, engaging framing, an editorial route, a reported experience | Independent consensus or permission to copy expression or format |
| Official source | Current rules, warnings, procedures and public-service facts within its scope | What travelers commonly feel or prefer |
| Provider terms | Exact coverage, price, exclusion or procedure for that provider and date | A market-wide rule |
| Native, resident or specialist reviewer | Idiomaticity, local context, practical nuance or specialist review | Evidence outside the reviewer's actual scope |

The appearance of a fact across independent programs, blogs, traveler reports and official
material is a signal that the topic may deserve prominence. Record the underlying fact in
original wording and preserve the source trail. Do not reproduce a source's script, sequence,
signature comparison, photograph, map, clip or trade dress.

## Research depth and stopping rule

Work in batches. A full first-country dossier normally starts with 30 or more relevant
firsthand accounts across at least three independent communities, then expands where important
topics remain thin or disputed. A complex destination may require 50–100 accounts. These are
floors and planning ranges, not evidence that quality rises mechanically with source count.

Prefer accounts about trips taken or published within the last 24 months. Older material may
support durable patterns but must not carry current prices, access, laws, services, insurance
terms or conditions.

Continue research when a high-impact topic has only one viewpoint, one platform, missing trip
context, unresolved contradictions, or no authoritative check where one is required. Stop a
research cycle when two consecutive, meaningfully different source batches add no new
high-impact topic or decision factor, every core topic has evidence from at least two source
communities, and disagreements are represented rather than averaged away. Record the batches
and stopping judgment.

This process detects saturation in the researched corpus; it does not make the corpus a
representative survey.

## Anecdotes that help decisions

An anecdote is useful when it preserves four things:

1. **Context:** season, route, traveler profile and relevant choice.
2. **Event:** what the traveler actually encountered.
3. **Action:** what they did before or after the event.
4. **Outcome:** cost, inconvenience, relief, regret or changed plan.

For a contested decision, seek contrasting outcomes. A car-insurance briefing might include:

- travelers who reported gravel, glass or wind-related damage and those who completed similar
  routes without damage while taking precautions;
- travelers whose card issuer confirmed coverage and others whose issuer excluded Iceland,
  a damage type, or reimbursement conditions;
- a documented claim that was reimbursed and a claim that was denied, when both can be found;
- the exact rental or card terms that governed each story, when available.

Do not fabricate symmetry. If only one side was found, say so. Paraphrase anecdotes and link
the source; a short quotation is exceptional and must be necessary. Do not elevate a vivid
story into `common` or attach a numerical likelihood without suitable data. Likewise, say that
a no-damage traveler *used* certain precautions, not that those precautions *prevented* damage,
unless the evidence can support the causal claim.

## Synthesis labels

Reader-facing content should make its epistemic role legible without sounding academic:

- **Travelers repeatedly mention:** a recurring theme across independent firsthand sources.
- **Experiences vary:** credible contrasting outcomes or preferences.
- **Our take:** an editorial recommendation for the stated audience and trip profile.
- **Officially:** a current authoritative rule or instruction.
- **Check before booking:** a provider-, date- or condition-specific fact the user must verify.
- **One traveler's experience:** a useful anecdote that is not a broader pattern.

Research notes keep the exact source and strength; reader copy may express the label naturally.

## Decision-support pattern

A useful section answers the decision rather than ending with a disclaimer:

1. Open with the surprising or consequential point.
2. Explain what several travelers encountered, including meaningful contrasting anecdotes.
3. Identify why outcomes differed: season, route, provider, preparation, risk tolerance or luck.
4. Separate official requirements and provider terms from traveler opinion.
5. Give a short checklist the user can complete before departure.
6. State the remaining choice and trade-off in neutral language.
7. Link the live source the traveler should use when the answer can change.

`Make your own decision` is not enough by itself. The preceding material must make that
decision materially easier.

## Voice and engagement

Write like a well-traveled friend who researched the details and respects the reader's agency.
The voice may be opinionated, especially where the target audience shares a recurring bias or
expectation, but label judgment and show the relevant context.

Prefer scenes, consequences and choices over encyclopedia summaries. Useful recurring forms
include:

- `What surprised travelers`
- `Decide this before landing`
- `Worth it if / skip it if`
- `Budget path / comfort path`
- `What went wrong`
- `Would they do it again?`
- `If you have half a day`
- `What you may hear or see there`

Use photography, maps, licensed short media, audio and place-name pronunciation where they
materially improve understanding. Borrow storytelling principles from successful travel media,
not protected wording, footage, graphics or distinctive episode structures.

## Practice

Practice is a separate, opt-in mode exposed when the pack has suitable language material.
It may offer listening, matching, recall and short scenario exercises. Do not:

- force a question after each information card;
- require practice to unlock reference content;
- create trivial distractors to satisfy a quota;
- imply that quiz completion proves travel readiness;
- use streak pressure, hearts or guilt copy.

An inline choice is justified only when comparing plausible actions itself teaches a useful
decision. It must be skippable.

## Sensitive and changing topics

Safety-, health-, emergency-, legal- and insurance-sensitive content needs authoritative or
contract-specific support as well as traveler experience. The content may tell a vivid story
and give a practical precaution, but it must distinguish current instructions from editorial
interpretation and send changing conditions to a live source.

For insurance and other personal risk choices, explain reported outcomes, exclusions,
deductibles, reimbursement mechanics and questions to ask. Do not promise coverage, estimate
an incident rate from forum posts, or choose the user's risk tolerance.

## Write, review, test, repeat

Treat content like code: sources are inputs, synthesis is design, prose is implementation,
and gates produce evidence.

1. **Research:** build the corpus and topic map; record context and contradictions.
2. **Synthesize:** write evidence statements and select decision-useful anecdotes before prose.
3. **Write:** create briefings and playbooks in original language, linked to the evidence.
4. **Evidence review:** verify that every material claim has the right kind of support and
   that anecdotes have not become statistics.
5. **Editorial review:** test usefulness, voice, pacing, audience fit, balance and engagement.
6. **Traveler-task test:** give a reviewer a realistic decision to make and measure whether
   the content supplies options, trade-offs, preparation steps and a next action quickly.
7. **Revise:** convert each failure into a research, synthesis or writing change; rerun the
   gates. Preserve unresolved findings in the dossier.

The deterministic dossier audit is `python tools/content/audit_research.py <research-dir>`.
The full criteria and reporting procedure live in the repository verification skill. Passing
the audit does not replace native, destination, safety, rights or real-traveler review.
